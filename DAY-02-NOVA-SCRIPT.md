# Day 2 - Compare Entity Types

## Nova Grant video script

**Toolkit source:** Entity-selection and business-structure section

Welcome to Day 2. Yesterday, you selected a working direction for your business structure. Today, we are going to test that direction by comparing the sole proprietorship, the limited liability company, the C Corporation, and the S Corporation tax-election profile.

The goal is not to select the structure with the best marketing language. The goal is to understand the operating tradeoffs behind each choice.

A sole proprietorship is generally the simplest starting point. It begins when one person operates a business without forming a separate legal entity. The owner maintains full control, business income is generally reported by the owner, and the startup administration is minimal. The main tradeoff is personal risk. There is generally no legal separation between the owner and the business, so business debts, lawsuits, or penalties may reach the owner's personal assets.

An LLC creates a separate state-law entity when it is properly formed and maintained. It is common among small service businesses because it can balance liability protection, management flexibility, and manageable formalities. An LLC may have one or more members, and its federal tax classification can vary based on ownership and elections. That flexibility is valuable, but it does not remove the need for separate finances, accurate records, insurance, and disciplined operations.

A C Corporation is a separate legal entity owned by shareholders. It provides structured ownership, formal governance, and a clearer path for adding investors or raising capital. Those advantages come with increased administration and entity-level federal taxation. Corporate records, governance, compensation, distributions, and tax treatment require careful planning.

An S Corporation belongs in this comparison, but we must name it accurately. S Corporation status is generally a federal tax election available to an eligible corporation or other eligible entity. It does not replace the underlying LLC or corporation, and it does not create liability protection by itself. The underlying legal entity provides the legal separation.

When the election is valid, eligible income, losses, deductions, and credits generally pass through to shareholders for federal tax purposes. The election also brings eligibility rules, a separate tax return, payroll responsibilities, and reasonable-compensation requirements for shareholder-employees who perform services. It should be evaluated only after reviewing projected profit, compensation, ownership eligibility, state treatment, and administrative capacity.

Now compare all four profiles across seven decision areas: what the option is, legal separation, personal liability, federal tax profile, ownership, administration, and growth fit.

If your highest priority is simplicity and you expect to remain a one-owner operation, a sole proprietorship may be part of the discussion, but you must understand the personal-liability tradeoff.

If you want meaningful legal separation with flexibility and manageable administration, an LLC may deserve closer review.

If you expect formal ownership, investors, significant scaling, or advanced governance, a C Corporation may fit the long-term plan.

If you expect an eligible owner-operated business to produce enough consistent profit to support payroll and the additional compliance, an S Corporation election may deserve professional analysis. It is not automatically the lowest-tax option, and it is not a substitute for an LLC or corporation.

Remember: a legal entity and its federal tax classification are related, but they are not always identical. An LLC can keep its state-law identity while receiving a different federal tax classification. Do not make an election based only on a social-media claim about tax savings.
