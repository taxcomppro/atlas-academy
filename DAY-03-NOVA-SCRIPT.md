# Day 3 - Evaluate Liability and Tax Impact

## Nova Grant video script

**Presenter:** Nova Grant, Launch Commander
**Format:** HeyGen Video Agent, 16:9 landscape
**Target length:** 3-5 minutes
**Tone:** Warm, confident, practical, and educational
**Toolkit source:** Liability protection and tax-impact section

Welcome to Day 3. You have compared the entity profiles. Now we are going to connect that choice to two things that affect every tax office owner: liability exposure and tax impact.

Start with the most important principle. Protection is a system, not a filing receipt.

An LLC or corporation can create legal separation between personal finances and business obligations when it is properly formed, maintained, and operated. That protection depends on disciplined behavior. Separate banking, accurate records, required filings, clear contracts, and consistent entity formalities all matter.

Entity protection also has limits. A tax professional can still be personally responsible for their own negligence, fraud, due-diligence failures, or other noncompliant conduct. The business structure must work alongside professional liability insurance and strong compliance controls.

Think in five layers: the legal entity, separate finances, accurate records, insurance, and compliance systems. Weakness in one layer can increase risk across the entire office.

Now connect the legal structure to the federal tax profile.

A sole proprietor generally reports business activity directly on the owner's individual return. Net earnings may be subject to self-employment tax. The reporting is comparatively simple, but there is no separate legal entity.

An LLC is a state-law legal form with federal tax flexibility. A single-member LLC is generally disregarded for federal income-tax purposes unless a valid election applies. A multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless another valid classification applies. The LLC's legal identity and its federal tax classification are related, but they are not the same decision.

A C Corporation is generally a separate federal taxpayer. The corporation may pay tax on its income, and shareholder distributions may create another tax layer. Compensation, benefits, retained earnings, distributions, and state taxes require careful planning.

An S Corporation election allows eligible income, losses, deductions, and credits to generally pass through to shareholders. But it also brings eligibility restrictions, a separate tax return, payroll responsibilities, and reasonable-compensation requirements for shareholder-employees who perform services.

An S election is not automatically the lowest-tax option. The analysis must consider projected profit, owner compensation, payroll costs, state treatment, administrative capacity, and whether the eligibility rules can be maintained.

Complete the Liability and Tax Impact Map. Document your risk level, financial separation, insurance status, compensation plan, projected profit consistency, and state review. Then list the questions a qualified professional must answer before you form an entity or make an election.

When your impact map and checkpoint are complete, Day 4 will unlock. Day 4 will prepare you to secure your Employer Identification Number.

Build the protection system. Document the tax questions. I will meet you at the next mission.
